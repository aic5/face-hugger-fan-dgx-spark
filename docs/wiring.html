02 / Power distribution

USB-C board, Pico and boost converter

The supplied product image identifies an ANMBEST JRC-B008 female USB-C breakout, with pads marked G, D+, D- and V. Here, G means GND and V is USB VBUS. It appears to be a passive connector breakout, not a voltage regulator, boost converter or PD trigger. This circuit requires 5 V at V, measured before connecting VSYS. User-supplied product link.

USB-C compatibility is not confirmed. A basic USB-C power-sink receptacle needs a separate 5.1 kΩ pull-down from CC1 to GND and another from CC2 to GND. Do not join CC1 and CC2 together. The supplied image shows an R1 area, but does not establish the values or presence of both independent pull-downs. These CC contacts are not among the four exposed pads. Do not assume that adding one resistor at R1 fixes the board. Renesas USB-C sink guidance.

Before connecting the Pico or boost: confirm the CC circuit with the supplier's schematic or measurements on the unpowered board. Then test V-to-G using the intended DGX USB-C cable in both plug orientations. Expect approximately 5 V; no voltage or orientation-dependent power can indicate a CC problem. Successful power-up alone does not certify the board or establish available current. Use a breakout with documented independent CC pull-downs if this one cannot be verified.

The image's 20 V / 3 A figures are connector ratings, not a promised output voltage or permission to draw 3 A. The four exposed pads provide a USB 2.0-style data connection, not SuperSpeed lanes, regardless of the listing's USB 3.1 wording. The live Amazon page could not be retrieved; board-specific observations here come from your supplied product image.

VSYS supply protection: the added Schottky diode prevents a later Pico USB power source from feeding back into the external V rail through VSYS. It is additional to the diode already on the Pico. Select a diode rated for Pico peak current, with suitable reverse-voltage margin. VSYS allows 1.8–5.5 V; this build uses verified 5 V before the diode, never the boost's 12 V. Pico W power guidance.

USB data assembly: TP2/TP3 are small solder pads. Keep the data pair short, routed together and away from the boost converter; provide strain relief and verify USB enumeration. For Wi-Fi-only operation, leave D+/D- individually insulated. Alternatively use a proper micro-USB plug breakout for data rather than soldering the pads; do not mix the two data paths.

One USB host at a time. While D+/D- are attached to the DGX through the USB-C board, do not plug a computer into the Pico's micro-USB socket. The socket and test pads share the same USB bus. A VSYS diode protects power sources, not against two USB hosts being connected together. Disconnect the breakout's data connection before using the socket for programming.

Check the current budget

The standard fan is rated up to 0.13 A at 12 V / 1.56 W. At an assumed 85% converter efficiency, the fan alone draws about 0.37 A at 5 V, plus Pico consumption and startup margin. This is an estimate, not a measured load. Fan ratings.

Verify the USB source's available current, breakout/cable limits and converter continuous/startup ratings. The JRC-B008's CC termination remains unverified; the connector's 3 A rating does not establish the source's current allowance. Do not use Pico's 3.3 V output to power the motor.

Keep 12 V off the USB rail

The boost output feeds only the motor branch. A USB-PD trigger requesting more than 5 V is not equivalent to this arrangement. Never feed that voltage into the Pico's USB socket or power pins.

Preserve a proper USB data path to the Pico. Do not splice or parallel USB data lines to create a hub.

Unresolved hardware detail: power sequencing. The fan's PWM input has an internal pull-up. With direct GPIO wiring, do not leave the fan powered while the Pico is unpowered. For bench tests, enable the fan supply only after the Pico is running; disable it before unplugging, resetting or flashing the Pico. One shared USB source alone does not guarantee this sequence. Before unattended use, verify sequencing or design a suitable power-off-tolerant signal interface. The proposed switch above is not assumed to be installed.

Interface basis: Noctua PWM electrical specification. The sequencing precaution is a design constraint for this direct-GPIO circuit, not a claim that the fan has a built-in sequencing feature.

03 / Board orientation

Find the physical Pico pins

Component side facing you, micro-USB connector at the top. Physical pin numbers are not GPIO numbers: GP15 is physical pin 20, not pin 15.

Earlier wiring correction: tach feedback now uses GP13, not the old GP14 assignment. CircuitPython pulse counting uses a separate PWM slice from GP15; keep GP12/GP13 free of other PWM uses.

04 / RPM measurement circuit

RPM feedback and PWM are different wires

Green: measured rotation

The fan's open-collector tach output gives two pulses per revolution. Pull it up only to the Pico's 3.3 V rail. At 50 pulses/second, measured speed is 1,500 RPM. Noctua currently recommends an external 1 kΩ pull-up and a 1 µF non-polarized capacitor to ground when noise affects RPM readings. Noctua RPM guidance.

The firmware always attempts to count GP13 falling edges. Missing feedback cannot distinguish a stopped fan, missing motor power, an open tach wire, or a bad pull-up/ground connection.

Blue: requested speed

The current firmware outputs direct 3.3 V, 25 kHz PWM. No external pull-up or inverting transistor is part of this design. Noctua recommends push-pull drive; do not substitute a generic open-collector circuit without reviewing the interface. PWM drive specification.

Control the blue signal, not the yellow motor supply. PWM percentage is not an exact RPM target; the current code does not perform closed-loop RPM regulation.

05 / Commissioning

First power-up checklist

  1. Unplug all power. Confirm the fan label says NF-A14 PWM, 12 V. Check connector numbering, secure terminals and insulate exposed conductors.
  2. Verify the power wiring. Confirm USB-C V is 5 V before connecting the Pico. With power off, check diode polarity, ground continuity and no accidental connection from boost OUT+ to a Pico rail. Do not use continuity mode on a powered circuit.
  3. Test the converter separately. Keep fan and Pico disconnected. Power the converter and measure a regulated +12 V between OUT+ and OUT-. Check polarity; disconnect power again before assembly.
  4. Connect the completed circuit. Start with the fan branch disabled. Power the Pico through VSYS from the verified USB-C 5 V branch and let the application boot, then enable the fan branch. Keep fingers, leads and tools clear.
  5. Check under load. Verify 12 V remains stable at the fan and there are no Pico resets, overheated parts or loose connections. If anything is abnormal, remove power.
  6. Test speed control. Use the existing dashboard to request full speed, a middle duty and off. Allow for the startup boost. A command timeout can restore full speed; this is not necessarily a wiring fault.
  7. Check RPM. Compare the reported RPM at full and middle speed. If it remains zero, verify the shared ground, green wire, 1 kΩ pull-up, and 1 µF filter; do not assume a PWM display proves physical rotation.

What to expect: the standard NF-A14 PWM is specified for 0 RPM at 0% PWM and up to 1,500 RPM at full speed without the Low-Noise Adaptor. Off is a speed command, not electrical isolation: the yellow wire still carries 12 V. Model specifications.

06 / Hardware vs. software status

What works now, what comes next

Now: Wi-Fi dashboard, PWM commands and automatic tach counting on GP13. The USB-C V branch supplies Pico power through VSYS. A working USB data connection also provides a filesystem connection and a debug console; V and GND alone do not. The firmware starts at full PWM and normally returns to full PWM after its command timeout.

DGX integration: the included Linux service reads CPU/GPU temperature and renews automatic fan commands over Wi-Fi. A USB cable alone does not provide temperature-based control; there is no USB serial command protocol.

Before unattended use: validate the actual boost/USB hardware, power sequencing and thermal behaviour. The existing software fallback does not guarantee cooling through loss of power, a reset or a frozen processor.
